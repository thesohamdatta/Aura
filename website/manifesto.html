Essay

The Third Device Hypothesis

June 2026 — Pune, India

I. The Device Problem

The personal computer arrived and gave us a stationary interface — powerful, precise, anchored to a desk. It extended what we could do, but it required us to go to it. The smartphone came next. It was the first device that came with us, that fit in a pocket, that was always within reach. And for two decades, we called this progress.

But the smartphone was never neutral. It was designed to demand attention. Every notification, every glowing rectangle, every moment of friction between a thought and its execution — these were not bugs in the system. They were the system. The interface became the cage.

We have been so focused on what smartphones can do that we stopped asking what they cost. The cost is not money. It is attention. Every second you spend looking at a screen is a second you are not present in the world that surrounds you.

II. The Third Device

There is a third category. Not stationary. Not mobile-and-demanding. Worn. Ambient. Screenless. A device that gathers context without requiring your gaze. That answers questions without requiring your hands. That listens without waiting for a command.

Clark and Chalmers argued in 1998 that cognition extends beyond the skull — that a notebook, a map, a person who remembers for you can all be part of a cognitive system. They called this the Extended Mind. If cognition can extend into a notebook, it can extend into a pendant. The question is what kind of extension we want.

Tulving's work on episodic memory shows that the human capacity for recall is associative and contextual, not indexed. We remember by reliving. An AI that captures the ambient context of a day — not as surveillance, but as a prosthesis — can become part of how we recall and reason.

III. The Hypothesis

"The third device is not a smarter phone. It is a different kind of thing entirely. It does not ask for your eyes. It does not ask for your hands. It asks only that you speak."

Fogg's Behaviour Model tells us that behaviour changes when motivation, ability, and a prompt converge. The smartphone prompt is a notification — it interrupts. The wearable prompt is a word — you initiate. The difference is not technical. It is relational.

Aura does not solve this problem. It is a prototype. The battery lasts one to two hours. The case is printed plastic. The AI is cloud-hosted and costs real money to run. We state this plainly because honesty about limitations is the only way to understand where to go next.

But the question it raises is real: what would it mean to carry intelligence without staring at it? What would it mean to have a device that is present without being demanding? What would change if the interface stepped back?

"We were not like this before."
